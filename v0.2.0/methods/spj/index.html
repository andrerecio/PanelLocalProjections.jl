Split-panel jackknife

The fixed-effects (within) estimator of a panel local projection is biased in panels with a short time dimension (Nickell 1981). In a local projection the bias is present even without a lagged dependent variable. At $h ≥ 1$ the error is a moving average of future innovations, so it is not strictly exogenous, and the within transformation brings future values of the regressors into every observation. The bias is $O(1/T)$ and distorts tests even when $N$ and $T$ grow together (Mei, Sheng & Shi 2026, Proposition 1). For a persistent shock ($\rho > 0$) it attenuates the response and grows with $h$ and with the persistence (Remark 3).

SplitPanelJackknife removes the leading term:

\[\hat\theta^{\,spj}_h = 2\hat\theta_h - \tfrac12\big(\hat\theta_{h,a} + \hat\theta_{h,b}\big)\]

$\hat\theta_h$ is the within estimate on horizon $h$'s sample, and $\hat\theta_{h,a}$, $\hat\theta_{h,b}$ are the within estimates on its two halves (Mei, Sheng & Shi 2026, eq. 16).

r = panellp(@formula(leads(y) ~ x + lags(x, 1:4) + lags(y, 1:4) + fe(id)), data;
            id = :id, time = :t, horizon = 10, estimator = SplitPanelJackknife())
summarize(r, Clustered(:unit; ssc = :none))   # the paper's unit-clustered variance

The halves

The split is made per unit, on each horizon's own estimation sample. A unit's rows are taken in time order:

  • split = :ceil (default) puts the first $\lceil n_i/2\rceil$ rows in half $a$, as the authors' code and Stata xtlp do;
  • split = :floor puts the first $\lfloor n_i/2\rfloor$ there, as in the paper's $\mathcal T_a = \{t \le T_h/2\}$.

The two rules differ only when $n_i$ is odd, and the choice moves published numbers: on the Romer–Romer GDP sample the paper's own split puts the Table 1 peak at −6.606, not −6.285 (see the replication page). Appendix B.2.4 splits each unit's contiguous span of an even length. The per-unit count split, which is the rule of the authors' code and of xtlp, extends this to odd counts and to gaps inside a unit. Every fixed effect is re-estimated inside each half, including time effects, which are then specific to the half (appendix B.2.3). The halves never drop rows. Under exact absorption a unit with a single row in a half contributes nothing to that half's estimate. Under OneSweep() with time effects, its demeaned zero still enters the period means of its half, as in the authors' code. A regressor that the fixed effects absorb inside a half (but not in the full sample) leaves that half rank-deficient; the horizon is then :spj_half_singular, with NaN coefficients.

With fixed effects other than the unit, the singleton policy changes the halves. A row that is a singleton in another dimension, a period observed for one unit only say, is absorbed in the full sample but still counts in its unit's $n_i$. So singletons = :drop (as xtlp) and :keep (as the authors' R code) give different SPJ estimates. With unit effects alone they agree.

Each horizon absorbs three samples: the full one and its two halves. With two-way effects the thinner halves need more sweeps, so SPJ takes two to five times as long as WithinFE; on the 1.1-million-row benchmark (firm and sector × quarter effects, 21 horizons) it takes about 5×.

Inference

The variance is a sandwich with the full-sample bread $(\tilde X'\tilde X)^{-1}$, the SPJ residuals $\tilde y - \tilde X\hat\theta^{spj}$ on the full-sample within data, and the scores $d = 2\tilde X - \tilde X_{\text{half}}$. Here $\tilde X_{\text{half}}$ holds each row within-transformed inside its own half. On a balanced panel with an even number of periods and unit effects, $d$ is the paper's $d^*$ (Assumption 2): $W_{it} - \bar W_{i,b}$ in half $a$ and $W_{it} - \bar W_{i,a}$ in half $b$. With time effects as well, it is the two-way $d^*$ of appendix B.2.3, that expression minus its cross-sectional mean at $t$. The test suite checks both, with variances that tell the scores apart row by row. For an odd $n_i$, $d = W_{it} - 2\bar W_i + \bar W_{i,\text{own half}}$, as in the authors' code and xtlp.

Every variance specification applies unchanged. The paper uses unit clustering without a small-sample factor (Clustered(:unit; ssc = :none)). The SPJ and FE estimators have the same asymptotic variance, but SPJ intervals can be wider in finite samples.

The authors' rule of thumb (§2.3) is to use SPJ when $T ≥ 30$ and $N/T ≤ 10$. Their theory requires $N/T^3 → 0$ and a fixed $h$.
