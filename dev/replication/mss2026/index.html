Mei, Sheng & Shi (2026)

Nickell bias in panel local projection: Financial crises are worse than you think, Journal of International Economics, 104210.

The four applications use the authors' replication data. They are not redistributed with the package; the authors publish them on GitHub and Mendeley. The test item MSS applications in test/test_mss_apps.jl runs all of them when the data sit under docs/references/code/panel-lp-replication/applications/.

The one-sweep approximation

The authors' code demeans by country and then by half-year once. That is the exact two-way within transformation only when the sample is balanced, which the GDP sample is. There OneSweep() and exact alternating projections agree to rounding (about 1e-14). The unemployment sample is unbalanced, and the published numbers depend on the approximation. Exact two-way fixed effects give (× 7; standard errors clustered by country):

Stata's xtlp …, tfe, whose two-way demeaning iterates, gives the exact columns too: to 1e-13 on the coefficients and 2e-10 on the standard errors, on both Romer–Romer samples and at every horizon (a local test item).

The paper's conclusion is unchanged: SPJ responses exceed FE from $h = 2$ on (at $h = 0, 1$ they are slightly smaller, in both versions). But the peak SPJ response is about 3% smaller, and the $h = 10$ response about 6% smaller. The SPJ standard errors depend on the approximation even more: with exact two-way fixed effects they are 12–18% smaller, while the FE ones move by at most 1%.

The split rule

The authors' code (like xtlp) gives the first $\lceil n_i/2\rceil$ rows of a unit to the first half. The paper defines the halves as $t \le T_h/2$, which is $\lfloor n_i/2\rfloor$. With an odd number of periods the two differ, and the Romer–Romer GDP sample (balanced, $T_h = 65$ at the Table 1 horizon) is affected. SplitPanelJackknife(split = :floor), the paper's rule, gives −6.606 at $h = 7$ instead of the published −6.285, and −5.476 instead of −5.052 at $h = 9$ (× 7).
